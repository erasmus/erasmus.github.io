Since around 2000 or so, every year has been the _year of Linux_, the moment the People finally start to _think different_ and throw off the Apple/Microsoft yoke. That, needless to say, was more fantasy than prophecy. It turns out that building one of the core user experiences of modern life ain't easy: it demands a concentration of energy and coordination rarely found in decentralised open-source communities. Now, fuelled by the rizz of [David Heinemeier Hansson](https://dhh.dk) and the deep pockets of tech millionaires, Omarchy has arrived on the scene with the explicit mission to expand the circle of Linux beyond its kernel of software savants to a broader congregation of "[the technically inclined](https://learn.omacom.io/3/omacom/81/doctrine)". A worthy pursuit, and an entirely plausible one, chaperoned as we now are by machines of loving grace. The question is whether this open-source OS could find its way into the mainstream, onto the machines and into the lives of the decidedly technically _disinclined_.

Omarchy is a portmanteau of _omakase_ and Arch Linux. Omakase is Japanese for _I'll leave it up to you_: let the chef serve what they think is worth your while. On this take-it-or-leave-it principle, DHH built Ruby on Rails, which, I think it's fair to say, has done rather well for itself. Clearly, the man has taste and a flair for the dramatic. So too with Omarchy, an Arch-based Linux distro: it has an über-geeky synthwave aesthetic, comes with a suite of default apps installed (_There's zero bloat here: Just everything I use_, as DHH put it in the [manual](https://omarchy.org/manual)), and offers a GUI experience based on tiling and workspaces through the window manager [Hyprland](https://hypr.land). Omarchy pursues distinction, definitely not imitation.

> 'Linux has been waiting for its big breakthrough for many years, but it won't happen on its own. There were some geeks who thought that if you just made it technically sound and free, people would come on their own. No matter how user-unfriendly it was. That's not how it works. People want something cool. Something that does something new — not a Temu version of Windows.'

That's how DHH [explains it to a Danish news site](https://finans.dk/tech/ECE19677002/dansk-techstjerne-samler-150-mio-kr-ind-for-at-kunne-give-alle-et-gratis-styresystem/). I felt seen. A decade ago I ran Ubuntu for a hot minute, then threw the keyboard out of the window and bought a MacBook. Windows had at times been annoying, Ubuntu was an _ordeal_. The system needed constant babysitting. Few things flowed. Programs crashed. My precious suite of tools was replaced by inferior, duct-taped garage builds. These interfaces were not retro-suave, they were plain kitsch, hard to love for anybody except their creators. I possessed neither the patience nor the grace to keep the thing alive. It made me sad.

## A wave forming

When I installed Omarchy some six months ago, I was instantly smitten. Everything worked — or, rather, my AI agent could make everything work. Monitor flickering on the boot screen? Ask Claude to fix it. Done. No battery status in the Bluetooth menu? Fix it. Done. The friction is gone, and what remains is the unadulterated joy of making things appear in the world. Homo faber in the virtual. Building stuff is exciting, but I'm not wedded to process for its own sake. Coding is fun, puzzles are fun, doing things the [Long, Hard, Stupid Way](https://frankchimero.com/blog/2011/the-long-hard-stupid-way/) can be rewarding, but there is a peak on the landscape of creative pleasures that is seeing an idea shift the state of the universe to something slightly better. Omarchy captures precisely that spirit.

Omarchy is agentic in two senses. First, it is an agent-first environment: you're expected to use your agents to bend it to your tastes (for example, it ships with an Omarchy skill). Second, the OS itself is being built by agents. After decades of chiselling code by hand in a Vim terminal, DHH has gone full agent. At the [opening keynote of the most recent Rails World](https://youtu.be/vDjW_dRyKXY?t=2287), he declared to his disciples that handwritten code is dead.

The Omacom Foundation, the non-profit behind the project, has raised over [$20 million through a trickle of grassroots donations and the open faucets of Silicon Valley heavyweights](https://omarchy.org/patrons/): Patrick Collison, Jack Dorsey, Tobias Lütke and others. Meetups are spawning around the world. The community is still dominated by a group of excitable followers, more than eager to throw themselves into the deep end of the OS, but there's a good chance the circle will widen further still.

## OS for the masses

The desktop OS is one of the foundational human-computer interfaces of our times. New form factors keep arriving — so far, the phone and watch OS has been the most notable, but voice-only and headset systems are probably not far off — yet the desktop stays. It's the carpenter's workbench, the place of dedication, of worship. Since the dawn of the personal computer, the desktop has been dominated by the AppleSoft duopoly: today, Windows is installed on around 76% of desktops, macOS 17%, and Linux on 5% ([StatCounter](https://gs.statcounter.com/os-market-share/desktop/worldwide/)). There's been much thinking-out-loud about what it would take to make Linux a contender, a somebody, give it some class, but the task of creating a system for the _normies_ goes far beyond the wrangling of code and system design and deep into the messy realm of human psychology. That's murky territory.

Now that we've entered an era in which agents may multiply human output by a factor of ten, or perhaps a hundred, could there be a path for an OS like Omarchy to reach 10%, 20%, or even 30% of desktops? Switching costs are brutal. People spend much of their time with their operating system; they have predilections, muscle memory, a specific way of doing things and expectations of how things should work. Most people do not want to change their ways. They are fond of their ways. They'll in fact go to considerable lengths to _avoid_ changing their ways. A switch to a new OS, therefore, has to feel less like a migration and more like moving into a nicer flat on the same street. It seems that Omarchy will also come in a [kid-friendly version](https://youtu.be/sNJYFuUOXOQ?t=3771): no sudo, vetted package library, parental oversight, etc. Could this be taken further, could Omarchy also be tweaked to become an OS for the tech-apathetic? Picture a retired property lawyer or a tween Labubu content creator: what would it take for them to feel at home on, say, a friendly derivative distribution of Omarchy that we'll, for the sake of argument, call Oma-Tomo? (Tomo, as in the root of the Japanese word for friend, _tomodachi_.)

First, the basics. Installation and setup should be easy. Monitor, microphone, headset, keyboard, speakers, the IoT-enabled vacuum cleaner — all of it should connect with minimal fuss. Apps should be fast, responsive and robust. Software and hardware updates should arrive with boring regularity and not like an ambush. The OS as a whole should offer reasonable shelter from the pitiless barrage of attacks from bad actors. It must feel like a home: straightforward to navigate, easy to maintain, safe. Omarchy would remain the base OS, doing all the groundwork of maintenance, updates and security, on top of which Oma-Tomo would sit as a hand-holding OS for the less confident user.

## Holding hands

Omarchy takes some getting used to. Even with agents at my side, I still spend significant time fixing things, and I do it in the terminal — not a place most people want to go. As someone who loves the system, I don't mind, but this is a dealbreaker for most. Below is a non-exhaustive and rather random collection of obstacles that I believe would frustrate the technophobic, with rough outlines of approaches that might ease the pain.

**Saturated keybindings.** By default, there are so many global bindings that hitting a key combo feels risky: a few unfortunate presses and you've enabled scroll mode in the window manager, or zoomed in to 200% with no obvious way to zoom out, and you helplessly press and click things, while windows fly around, as you entangle yourself ever further in a trap of your own making. This is an undignified experience. A few patterns could help here.

- *Keep bindings to an absolute minimum.* A handful of menu opens and tiling operations, nothing more. More keybindings could emerge over time as users grow comfortable with the ways of the OS. 'You just opened [app] for the third time — do you want to assign a keybinding to make it easier to open in future?'
- *Introduce a universal undo.* Ctrl+Z is one of the most brilliant features of the digital world: where else can you, mere mortal, traverse the decision tree bidirectionally? Wherever possible, let users reverse what they just did: a single global undo that takes back the last action. Accidentally enabled zoom? Ctrl+Z. Undo. You're now back where you came from. This becomes the fallback people learn alongside every other command, and it provides psychological safety as they punch buttons.
- *Guard destructive actions.* Anything that destroys data should be blocked or confirmed by default.

**Window tiling.** The Hyprland window manager is excellent but unusual. The basic commands are easy; the trouble is that the primary interaction runs through workspaces, not the program-first navigation around which macOS and Windows are structured. People are used to dragging windows about with a mouse. A first-run onboarding, and suggestions as you go, would turn a strange concept into an intuitive one.

**Show what is running.** People expect to reach running programs from some kind of menu. Without much overhead, programs could be pinned or dragged to the menu so they stay reachable from any window, giving a quick sense of everything that is open and a way back to the window you came from.

**Make moving a file simple.** It is surprisingly complicated: open the file manager in another window, drag files across. Finding an intuitive gesture for this would do a great deal of good.

Above are a few cherry-picks to give an idea of the design challenges that stand in the way of broad adoption. Each is a hard problem in its own right. Solving them all would take coordination, wisdom, strategy, flair — in short, a band of expert designers collaborating at scale inside a loosely connected community. Yesterday, such a design effort would have meant far too many cooks in the kitchen, but perhaps we're in a new era, in which taste can evolve in unison across time and space.

## Benevolent design dictators and the swarm

The $20 million raised to build Omarchy is chicken feed next to the billions Apple and Microsoft pour into their flagship OSs. A single year of their OS budgets dwarfs everything the Omacom Foundation has raised, which means that development of Omarchy would have to be something like a hundred times more efficient to compete. That would entail removing the friction that emerges in large-scale collaborative efforts. A big enterprise is full of wasteful bureaucracy. Like a space rocket whose mass is mostly the fuel it must burn to escape gravity, the accumulated transaction costs of thousands of employees have historically been the price of building mega-projects. Maybe AI is the anti-gravity sauce.

Loosely organised groups of open-source contributors have created a stupefying amount of value over the years, yet most projects never acquire the polish that makes software accessible. Maintainers of successful FOSS projects skew towards shape-rotators, who take pride in their ability to navigate difficult systems and carry some disdain for the people who do not master the same abilities. VideoLAN is an awesome feat of engineering, built by hundreds of contributors under the suzerain [Jean-Baptiste Kempf](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nepKKz-MzFM), but VLC's GUI certainly isn't a pretty thing. The most successful open-source projects evolve under a charismatic leader who can argue points of technical minutiae, and that works because code is either right or wrong: it compiles or it doesn't, it introduces a bug or it doesn't, it slows a process or it doesn't. While good design does indeed emerge from some principles that hold true in all contexts, it's also about taste, that elusive, fuzzy, shape-shifting factor. And designers — those who really care about such amorphous things — have been excluded from meaningfully contributing to these projects, for want of engineering flair and because consistent design is hard to draw from distributed collaboration between dozens or hundreds of designers. The lack of a design dictator means slippage, inconsistency — in short, shitty design.

Now there may be another way. Imagine the Oma-Tomo design principles distilled into a set of maxims that an AI design ~~dictator~~ director — the benevolent supremo as a model — can use to gatekeep the pull requests of agent swarms belonging to thousands of design contributors. The Director wouldn't need taste of its own. Its job is consistency: applying the principles uniformly across a vast range of concepts and proposals, so that the thousandth contribution is judged by the same standard as the first. Taste stays with the humans. A small, carefully curated committee of tastemakers guards the Oma-Tomo design principles with their lives and reputations. When a proposal can't be clearly decided by the principles, the Director doesn't guess; it surfaces the tension to the committee, which either arbitrates the case or decides whether the principles themselves warrant an update.

Consider the aforementioned idea of reducing the keybindings to an absolute minimum. A designer in the community proposes a concept that leans on the mouse for window management and opens a pull request with a design spec to formalise their proposal. More mouse use may well be fine — plenty of these users will reach for the mouse first — but the Oma-Tomo design principles state that every action must be possible by voice or keyboard alone. The point of Oma-Tomo isn't to abandon the keyboard but to make it approachable for the less tech-savvy. The proposal offers no keyboard path for several of its actions, so the Oma-Tomo Design Director agent sends it back with a request for changes.

On these categorical imperatives, the pool of contributors to the design of Oma-Tomo grows by coordinating a swarm of agents belonging to hundreds, or even thousands, of contributors — orchestrating, approving, rejecting, requesting changes, escalating, and ensuring the core design maxims hold true in every corner of the system. If such a Design Director could be built, it would deliver an operating system usable by mainstream humans and their agents alike, and a whole new class of contributor, the designers, might finally join the cause. At this point, the orders-of-magnitude capital advantage of AppleSoft may not matter all that much.

## Cracks in the duopoly wall

This is a unique moment in Linux history. A hypeman with both the capital, technical, and organisational skills for greatness has launched a distro that makes many, many people feel excited. It's hard not to be infected by the pure enthusiasm of DHH — p(bloom) all the way, baby. The stated goal is to bring Linux to technically minded people, the ones too impatient to sacrifice their scarce hours to reading the fucking manual. People like me. And I already love Omarchy. I think many others could too — with a few turns of the knobs, some softened edges, and, yes, making a couple of  apologies for Linux.

Building an OS for the masses would pitch an Omarchy derivative against macOS and Windows and their gargantuan coffers. With an AI Design Director and a human-curated set of design principles, it may be possible to fill the open-source kitchen with more chefs than ever to collaboratively construct a _homely_ UX, albeit one based on unusual design primitives. Combined with Omarchy's ability to change according to the whims/wishes of its direct user, this could be a attractive path for many. Maybe we're seeing the first rays of light through the cracks in the wall of the AppleSoft duopoly, of which we have all been vassals for a lifetime.
